Your first time on roller skates comes with a lot of questions. The most common one is simple: what should I put on before I go? Most people are not worried about the skating itself yet. They want to know what to bring so they feel ready when they walk through the door.

This article covers the parts of getting ready that published safety advice speaks to directly. That means how skates should fit, which protective gear helps, and what to do about falling, which every new skater does at least once. It does not try to tell you a dress code, because that is set by each rink and by what is comfortable for you.

## Start with the skates

Skates are the most important thing you will wear, so the fit matters more than anything else on the list. KidsHealth, a children's health education site, says skates should be comfortable and should be neither too big nor too small. A skate that is too loose lets your foot slide around inside the boot. A skate that is too tight can hurt before you have rolled a single lap.

The same source gives two habits worth copying from the first minute. Push your feet all the way back into the boot before you fasten anything, and always buckle your skates. Fastening the skates with your heel seated at the back keeps your foot where the boot expects it to be.

It also offers a quick test for ankle support. Press on the plastic of the boot. In its words, "If you can squeeze it, the material is not strong enough." Newer skaters lean on their ankles more than they expect, so support there is worth a moment of your attention when you pick up a pair.

## Protective gear that cushions a fall

Falls are the main reason protective gear exists, and a new skater should plan for them. KidsHealth recommends knee pads, elbow pads and wrist guards, and explains what they do: "These items help prevent scrapes and also cushion the bones in case of a fall."

Knees, elbows and wrists are the parts of the body that usually reach the floor first when a skater loses balance. That is why these three pieces are the standard set. If you are only going to get one of them, ask yourself which fall you worry about most, then think about covering the other two as soon as you can.

The source does not give detailed fit instructions for pads, so check the manufacturer's sizing on whatever you buy, and make sure you can still bend your knees and elbows comfortably once the pads are on.

## A helmet made for skating

KidsHealth says to use a helmet designed specifically for inline skating or skateboarding. It adds that the helmet should sit low in the back of the head, and that you should always fasten the chin strap snugly under your chin.

A helmet that is pushed back on the head or left loose is not doing the job it was made for. Take a minute to adjust the fit before you step onto the floor, and check the strap again if the helmet moves while you skate.

## Gloves and a mouthguard

Two smaller items come up in the same safety guide. Light gloves protect your fingers. A mouthguard, in the source's words, "will help protect your teeth and mouth in case of a crash or a fall."

Neither of these is as common as pads or a helmet, so many first-time skaters never think about them. They are both inexpensive pieces of gear compared with the problems they are meant to prevent, and they are worth considering if you want a fuller set of protection.

## Learn how to fall

Gear helps, but how you fall also matters. KidsHealth gives three pieces of advice. Practice falling on a soft surface, such as grass next to pavement. Crouch down as you fall, so you have less distance to tumble. And relax while falling instead of going stiff.

Roller skating instruction treats falling as a skill to learn. The Roller Skating Association International describes its Learn to Skate program as covering skills that range from "mastering the art of falling and getting back up" to gliding backwards. Its adult kit includes posters, a skills set, evaluation sheets and certificates made for adult learners. Both sources agree on the same idea: falling is part of learning, and you can practice it.

It can help to try the crouch on purpose, slowly, before you ever lean into a real fall. Your body will have a better chance of remembering what to do when the moment is sudden.

## Warm up before you skate

KidsHealth also says to always warm up and stretch before skating. Skating asks your legs, hips and ankles to hold you in a bent, balanced position for a long time, which can feel different from walking. A few minutes of stretching before you lace up is a small cost.

## Check with the rink before you come

Every rink sets its own rules about what skaters need to wear and what gear they can bring. Because those rules belong to the rink, the best way to avoid surprises is to ask before your visit. Find out what is required, what is allowed and what you should leave at home.

## A short plan for your first visit

Put the advice together and a first visit looks like this. Choose skates that fit, with your heel pushed to the back of the boot and every buckle fastened. Wear a helmet made for skating, with the chin strap snug. Add knee pads, elbow pads and wrist guards, and think about gloves and a mouthguard. Warm up, then practice crouching and relaxing as if you were falling, before you need it.

:::note Worth knowing
KidsHealth says knee pads, elbow pads and wrist guards both help prevent scrapes and cushion the bones in case of a fall.
:::

Nobody expects a first-time skater to be smooth. A few simple choices before you arrive make the first hour easier, and let you spend it learning instead of worrying about what you forgot.
