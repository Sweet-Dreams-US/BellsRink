If you have never skated, a roller skate can look like a boot with wheels bolted to it. That is close, but each part has a job. Knowing what the parts do helps first-time skaters feel less nervous on the floor, and it helps parents understand what their child is standing on.

This article walks through the main parts of a roller skate and how they work together to let a person glide.

## The boot holds your foot and your ankle

The boot is the part that connects you to the skate. It has to fit well, because every move you make passes through it.

The Moxi Shop buying guide explains that suede boots mold to your feet after a break-in period. Depending on the model, that break-in can take about 10 hours for some boots and about 20 for others. Ankle support also varies by model, and some boots add extra padding or a flexible ankle.

SkatePro adds that padding around the ankle and a firm tongue help with comfort on longer sessions. It also says boot stiffness depends on the type of skating. Stiffer boots suit skatepark and long-distance skating, and softer boots suit dancing and slalom.

:::note Worth knowing
Both guides treat the boot as something that is shaped by use. A new boot may need a break-in period, and the time depends on the model.
:::

## The plate holds everything under the boot

Under the boot is a base called the plate. SkatePro describes it as the base attached to the boot. It comes in different materials. Nylon is light and beginner-friendly but less durable. Aluminum is strong and heavier, and is aimed at advanced skaters. Fiberglass sits in the middle on weight and strength.

The plate matters because it gives the wheels something solid to attach to. A skater pushes down through the boot and the plate, and the plate passes that force to the wheels.

## Trucks and bearings let the wheels turn

Trucks are the metal axles connected to the plate that hold the wheels. SkatePro says beginner skates use standard trucks, while skates made for aggressive skating use larger ones.

Inside each wheel are bearings. Bearings let the wheel spin smoothly around the axle. SkatePro explains that bearings are rated on the ABEC scale from 1 to 9, which measures how precisely they are built. It also says the overall quality of a bearing often affects spin speed more than the ABEC number does. To keep bearings working well, SkatePro advises keeping them out of humid storage, drying them after wet skating, and cleaning and lubricating them from time to time.

## Wheels decide grip and speed

Wheels are what touch the floor, so they have a large effect on how a skate feels. Wheel hardness is measured on the durometer scale, written with the letter A after the number.

The Moxi Shop guide says softer wheels grip more, and harder wheels are faster and suit indoor or skatepark use. SkatePro gives ranges: soft wheels from 78A to 82A grip better and absorb shock, which suits outdoor cruising. Medium-hard wheels from 80A to 85A suit jam skating and roller dance. Hard wheels from 95A to 100A last longer and suit aggressive skating.

Size matters too. SkatePro says larger wheels, 58 to 65 millimeters for outdoor use, give more speed and roll better over cracks. Smaller wheels are easier to control, and it says beginners are better served by smaller wheels.

## The toe stop helps you slow down

At the front of many quad skates is a toe stop. SkatePro says bull-eye toe stops are fixed in place and suit beginners. Adjustable toe stops let a skater set the height and are recommended for intermediate and advanced skaters. Dancers and jam skaters often use jam plugs instead, which do not provide braking.

For a new skater, the toe stop is one of several ways to learn to slow down. A coach or instructor can show you how to use it.

## How the parts work together to glide

When you stand on a pair of skates, your weight pushes down through the boot, the plate and the trucks into the wheels. The bearings let each wheel spin freely, and the wheels roll across the floor. Because the wheels spin instead of dragging, a small push sends you gliding forward.

Balance is what keeps the glide steady. That is a skill, and skills are learned. The Roller Skating Association International runs a Learn to Skate Program for children and adults, taught by a certified Learn to Skate Instructor. It covers skills "from mastering the art of falling and getting back up to gliding effortlessly backwards." Falling and getting back up is part of what the program teaches, which is useful for any new skater or parent to know.

## A quick summary

A roller skate is a set of parts that each do one job. The boot holds the foot and ankle. The plate is the base under the boot. Trucks hold the wheels, bearings let them spin, and the wheels decide how much the skate grips and how fast it rolls. A toe stop can help with slowing down. With those parts in place, learning to balance and to fall safely is what turns a first-time skater into a confident one.
