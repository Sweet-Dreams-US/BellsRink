If you are new to roller skating, the wheels can look like the least interesting part of a skate. They are small, round and all look about the same. In fact the wheels change how a skate feels more than almost anything else. Two pairs of skates with the same boots and the same frames can feel completely different if one has soft wheels and the other has hard ones.

The main reason is the surface. A smooth floor and a rough sidewalk ask different things from a wheel. This lesson explains how wheels are rated, why wheels are sold as indoor or outdoor, and what that means when you pick skates for your first few visits to a rink.

## What wheel hardness means

Roller skate wheels are rated on a number called durometer. You will see it printed on the wheel as a number followed by the letter A, such as 85A. The letter tells you the scale. The Moxi Shop buying guide explains that wheel hardness is measured in durometer "A" and that it affects speed, grip and where you can skate.

A lower number means a softer wheel. A higher number means a harder wheel. Softer wheels press into the floor a little and grip more. Harder wheels roll faster and slide more easily, because less of the wheel touches the floor and it does not squish.

Makers do not all draw the lines in the same place. Riedell Skates describes outdoor wheels as running from 78A to 85A and indoor wheels from 88A to 101A. Deva Skation gives soft wheels as 78A to 84A, medium wheels as 85A to 91A and hard wheels as 92A to 100A. The numbers differ a little, but the pattern is the same: soft for rough ground, hard for smooth floors.

:::note Worth knowing
Moxi Shop says softer wheels grip more and suit outdoor skating, while harder wheels are faster and suit indoor or skate park use.
:::

## Why outdoor wheels are soft

Outdoor surfaces are not smooth. Riedell Skates points to concrete and asphalt that can have cracks, pebbles and debris. A soft wheel absorbs some of that. It rolls over small bumps instead of passing every one of them up through your boot and into your legs.

Soft wheels also grip better on pavement. That helps when you stop, turn or push off on a surface that is a little gritty or uneven. It is also why Moxi Shop notes that outdoor complete skates ship with soft wheels made for casual skating on all kinds of terrain.

The trade-off is speed. A soft wheel has more contact with the ground, so it takes more effort to keep rolling.

## Why indoor wheels are hard

A rink floor is a different place. Riedell Skates describes the indoor floor as smooth and usually free of debris, and mentions smooth maple floors as one example. On a surface like that, there are no cracks to absorb, so a soft wheel has little to do. What it does instead is hold on too well.

Riedell Skates puts it this way: "If you use soft wheels on a rink floor, you'll experience slower skating that almost feels sticky." Hard wheels glide on a smooth floor, and they also wear less, according to the same article.

Deva Skation says hard wheels in the 92A to 100A range are best suited to indoor rinks with smooth surfaces. That is the reason indoor wheels are sold as their own kind of wheel. They are made for one job, and they do it well on the right floor.

## What happens when you mix them up

Using the wrong wheel in the wrong place is a common beginner mistake, and it goes two ways.

Hard indoor wheels on pavement are the bigger concern. Riedell Skates says you will feel every crack and bump because a hard wheel does not absorb them. Deva Skation adds a warning that indoor wheels used outdoors will not stick to the pavement and might cause you to slide around or even fall. It also says this kind of use will damage the wheels.

Soft outdoor wheels on a rink floor are less risky but less pleasant. The skating feels slow and sticky, and it can be tiring because you work harder to keep your speed.

## Medium wheels and wheel size

Some wheels sit in the middle. Deva Skation lists medium wheels from 85A to 91A as a fit for both indoor and outdoor use. If you plan to skate in both places and only want one set of skates, a medium wheel is a reasonable compromise. It will not be perfect on either surface.

Wheel size matters too, though it is a smaller choice for a beginner. Deva Skation says smaller wheels, 57mm to 62mm, suit agility and quick turns, the kind of movement used in jam and derby skating. Larger wheels, 63mm to 70mm, suit long distances and higher speeds, though they accelerate more slowly.

## What a beginner should do

For a beginner, the Riedell Skates FAQ recommends soft wheels because of the forgiveness and grip they give while you are learning. Indoor wheels are the kind made for smooth rink floors, so if you plan to skate mostly at a rink, that is the kind to look for as you get more comfortable.

A few simple steps help you decide:

1. Decide where you will skate most. Smooth rink floors call for harder wheels. Sidewalks and paths call for softer ones.
2. Check the durometer number printed on the wheel, and remember that makers use slightly different ranges.
3. If you will skate in both places, consider a medium wheel.
4. Keep indoor wheels off pavement, since it can damage them.

## The short version

Wheels are rated by hardness. Soft wheels grip and absorb bumps, so they belong outside. Hard wheels glide and wear less, so they belong on smooth indoor floors. Pick the wheel for the surface you skate on, and the rest of your skating gets easier.
