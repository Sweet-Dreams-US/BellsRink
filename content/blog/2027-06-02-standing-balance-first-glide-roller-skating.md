Most people feel a little wobbly the first time they stand up on roller skates. That is normal. Skates roll, and your body has to learn that a floor can move under your feet. The good news is that a few simple habits make the first session much easier. This post covers how to stand, how to balance, how to fall safely and how to take your first glide.

## Start with the right gear

Before you roll anywhere, put on protective gear. Skates.com lists a helmet, wrist guards, elbow pads and knee pads. Roller Magic calls the same four pieces "the core four," and says that if a budget is tight, buy wrist guards first and a helmet second. ProSkaters Place describes wrist guards as non-negotiable and puts knee pads next.

Fit matters too. Skates.com and Roller Magic both say your heel should stay down when you bend your knees, and your toes should touch the front of the skate without curling.

:::note Worth knowing
All three skate guides suggest practicing first on grass or carpet, then moving to a smooth, flat surface. This lets you get used to the skates before they roll freely. (Sources: Skates.com, Roller Magic, ProSkaters Place)
:::

## The ready position

The ready position is the stance you come back to whenever you feel unsteady. Skates.com says to stand with your knees bent and your shoulders over your toes, keep your feet a little apart, and look ahead instead of at your feet. Roller Magic describes feet about shoulder-width apart, weight over the balls of the feet and eyes forward. ProSkaters Place says to bend your knees more than feels natural, enough that you can see your toes past your knees.

Bent knees are the key. Skates.com says a bent-knee stance lowers your center of gravity. Roller Magic says locked knees cause most wobbles. Both also say to keep your weight over the balls of your feet, since leaning back sends the skates out in front of you.

Arms help with balance. Roller Magic suggests holding your hands out in front at waist height, not pinned to your sides. ProSkaters Place suggests keeping your hands in front where you can see them, roughly at hip height.

## Practice balance before you move

Spend a minute or two in the ready position without moving. ProSkaters Place suggests standing in it for a full minute before going anywhere. Roller Magic suggests holding the bent-knee stance for 30 seconds on grass, then shifting your weight from one foot to the other without lifting your feet.

Then try marching in place. Skates.com and ProSkaters Place both start beginners with this. Lift one skate a few centimeters, set it down, and repeat with the other. It teaches you to move your weight without rolling away.

## Learn to fall before you need to

Falling is part of learning, and knowing how to do it makes it less scary. The Roller Skating Association's Learn to Skate program lists "mastering the art of falling and getting back up" among the things it teaches.

The skate guides agree on the basics. Bend your knees and get low, then fall forward onto your knee pads and let the wrist guards and elbow pads take the rest. Skates.com says to avoid falling backward onto your tailbone. ProSkaters Place suggests keeping your fingers curled into loose fists, and if you are going over backward, tuck your chin and try to sit down. Roller Magic says to bend deeply and rotate to land on a hip and pad, not on your spine.

You can practice dropping to your knees on grass or carpet. It feels odd at first, but it makes a real fall less of a surprise.

## Getting back up

Skates.com describes getting up this way: roll onto both knees, put one skate flat between your hands, push up on that knee with both hands, and bring the other skate alongside. Roller Magic gives a similar sequence from hands and knees. It says to bring one skate forward and flat, push through that knee and your wrists, stand up through the lead leg, and bring the back skate under your hips.

Do this a few times on a carpet or grass before you try it on a hard floor. A slow, practiced movement is easier to repeat when you are tired or nervous.

## Your first glide

Once you can stand and march in place, try a small glide. Skates.com suggests a duck walk forward on a smooth floor with your toes turned out in a V. After a few steps, glide on two feet. Roller Magic says to keep your strides small.

To push, ProSkaters Place says to push one skate out to the side at roughly 45 degrees, not straight back, pressing through the inside edge. Let the push carry your weight to the other skate, then bring the pushing foot back under you. Glide for a count of one, two, three. Skates.com describes the same idea: push one skate out to the side and slightly back, glide on the other, bring the pushing foot back and alternate.

:::note Worth knowing
Roller Magic says three short sessions a week beat one long one, and ProSkaters Place suggests 30 to 45 minutes per session. (Sources: Roller Magic, ProSkaters Place)
:::

## A word on stopping

Learn at least one way to stop early. Skates.com describes a toe-stop drag for quad skates: bend your knees, extend one leg behind you, lift that heel and press the toe stop into the floor. Roller Magic says steady pressure works and fast jabs do not. Another option is the plow stop, where you push your skates apart, turn your toes in and press out through your heels.

## Keep it short and keep it light

Your first session does not need to cover everything. Stand, balance, march, fall and get up, then try a short glide. If you do those few things well, the rest comes more easily. Take breaks, drink water, and stop while it is still fun.
