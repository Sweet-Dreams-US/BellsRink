If you are new to roller skating, you will fall at some point. That is normal. What helps is knowing how to fall in a way that protects you, and how to get back on your feet without panic.

The Roller Skating Association International lists "falling and getting back up" as part of its Learn to Skate program, right alongside skills like gliding backwards. Falling is treated as a skill to learn, not a failure. This article covers the basics so your first sessions feel less stressful.

## Falling is part of learning

Skating asks your body to balance on a moving surface with small wheels under each foot. When your balance slips, a fall can happen quickly. Skaters of every age and level have done it.

The goal is not to never fall. The goal is to fall in a way that lets you stand up, laugh, and keep going. Most of what protects you is simple: a relaxed body, bent knees, and the right gear.

## Stay low and stay loose

Several skate instructors and skate makers give the same first piece of advice. Stay relaxed. Roces says to relax your body to minimize impact, and Thisissoul says that tension can increase the risk of injury.

Bend your knees and keep your weight over your skates. Inmove Skates describes this as keeping your weight centered, and adds that if you begin to lose your balance, you should try to lean forward or to the side instead of backward.

A lower body position also means you have a shorter distance to fall.

## Falling forward

Forward falls are the ones many beginners fear. A few steps help.

Bend your knees as much as you can. Thisissoul says to drop onto your knees rather than catching yourself with your hands, and that knee pads with a hard plastic cap let you slide over the floor. Roces says to aim for your forearms, or slide on your knees if you are wearing pads.

Inmove Skates adds that you should tuck your chin toward your chest to protect your head, and keep your hands in front of you so your knee pads and wrist guards take the contact.

:::note Worth knowing
Thisissoul says that dropping onto padded knees is better than catching yourself with your hands, and that a sliding fall spreads out the force instead of stopping all at once.
:::

## Falling sideways or backward

A sideways fall can be guided. Thisissoul suggests lowering yourself down instead of letting the fall take over, with one hand to the side for balance.

Backward falls feel the most scary, and they call for calm. Inmove Skates says to tuck your chin, bend your knees, and aim to land on the padded, fleshy part of your lower back, with your arms close to your body. Thisissoul advises turning your shoulders slightly so the fall moves to the side, which helps avoid a direct hit on your back and neck, and says not to flail your arms. Roces says to tuck your chin and roll onto your shoulder to protect your head.

## What protective gear does

Protective gear does real work. A 1996 study in the New England Journal of Medicine looked at in-line skating injuries in the United States. It found that wrist injuries were the most common, at 32 percent of injuries, and that 25 percent of all injuries were wrist fractures.

The same study found that skaters who did not wear wrist guards had about 10 times the odds of a wrist injury compared with skaters who did. Skaters who did not wear elbow pads had about 9.5 times the odds of an elbow injury. The authors concluded that wrist guards and elbow pads are effective in protecting in-line skaters against injuries.

:::note Worth knowing
The study was about in-line skating, and the authors could not judge how well helmets worked. Roces recommends wrist guards, knee pads, and a helmet, and says gear also builds confidence while you learn.
:::

## Getting back up

The sources above explain falling in more detail than getting up, so the steps here are plain common sense. Take a moment after a fall. Check that nothing hurts before you move.

Then work your way to your hands and knees on the floor, keep your skates from rolling out behind you, and bring one foot flat under you at a time. Stand up slowly, with your knees bent, before you start to roll again. If you are on a busy floor, move toward the wall or the edge of the rink to catch your breath.

If something hurts more than you expected, stop skating for the day.

## Practice at your own pace

You do not need to master everything on the first visit. Spend a few minutes at the start of a session on bending your knees and keeping your weight centered. Move at a pace that feels safe. Falling gets less frightening as you gain control, and the skill of standing back up is one you will use many times.
