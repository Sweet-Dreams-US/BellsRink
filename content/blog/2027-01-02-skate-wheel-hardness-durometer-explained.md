If you have ever looked at a pair of roller skates and seen a number followed by the letter A on the wheel, you have seen the hardness rating. It is called durometer. It looks like a small detail, but it changes how a skate feels under your feet more than most people expect. This post explains what the number means, what it does to grip and speed, and how to think about it before you buy or borrow a pair of skates.

## What durometer measures

Durometer is a measure of how hard a wheel is. Skate wheels use the A scale, and the rule is simple. A lower number means a softer wheel. A higher number means a harder wheel. Riedell Skates describes it this way: "with lower numbers meaning softer and higher numbers meaning harder." Skates.com says the same thing in its own words: the higher the number, the harder the wheel.

Think of it the way you would think of the sole of a shoe. A soft rubber sole bends, grips and absorbs bumps. A hard sole is stiffer and slides more easily. Skate wheels work the same way, only the difference between them is easy to feel because you are rolling on them.

The Moxi Shop buying guide sums up what hardness changes: it "affects speed, grip, and where you can skate." Those are the three things to keep in mind. Hardness is not about good or bad. It is about matching the wheel to the job.

## The trade between grip and speed

Soft wheels grip more. Moxi puts it plainly: "Softer wheels grip more and are better outdoors," and "harder wheels are faster and better indoors or at the skate park."

That gives you the basic trade. A soft wheel presses into the floor a little and holds on. This helps you feel steady, and it helps on surfaces that are not perfectly smooth. The cost is that the wheel drags more, so it rolls slower.

A hard wheel does the opposite. It holds its shape, so it rolls farther and faster on a smooth floor. It also has less grip, which means it lets go more easily when you turn, push off at an angle or stop.

Neither one is the right answer for everyone. A new skater who wants to feel steady has different needs than a skater who wants to move quickly around a smooth floor.

## Typical numbers for indoor and outdoor wheels

Two sources give ranges you can use as a rough guide. Riedell says outdoor wheels usually fall somewhere between 78A and 85A, and indoor wheel hardness is usually between 88A and 101A. Skates.com gives a similar picture. It lists outdoor wheels at 78A or 84A, and indoor wheels at 90A to 101A.

The exact edges of the ranges differ a little from one source to the next. The pattern is the same everywhere. Outdoor wheels are softer. Indoor wheels are harder.

Skates.com adds a useful point about indoor wheels. Among indoor wheels, the softer ones grip better than the harder ones. So even inside the indoor range, there is a choice to make between more grip and more roll.

:::note Worth knowing
Moxi notes that each of its skates comes with soft wheels that can be used on all terrains for casual skating. Soft wheels are a common starting point because they work in more places.
:::

## Why the surface decides

The reason indoor and outdoor wheels differ is the floor. Outdoor ground has cracks, grit and small bumps. Riedell says outdoor wheels run softer "so they can absorb bumps, cracks, and debris on rougher surfaces." A rink floor is smooth, so a harder wheel can roll freely without having to soak up anything.

Using the wrong wheel shows up quickly. Riedell says soft wheels on a rink give you "slower skating that almost feels sticky." Hard wheels outdoors mean "you'll feel every crack and bump due to the lack of absorption."

If you have ever tried to skate on a surface that felt wrong, hardness may have been part of the reason.

## What this means for a beginner

Riedell suggests softer wheels for people who are learning: "Soft, generally, for forgiveness and grip while learning." The idea is that a beginner is still working out balance, and extra grip makes the skate feel less slippery.

Beginner skills matter more than gear, though. The Roller Skating Association International Learn to Skate Program is built for both children and adults, and it teaches basic skills, including "mastering the art of falling and getting back up." Good wheels will not replace that practice. They just make the first sessions a little easier.

If you are borrowing skates, you may not get to pick the wheel hardness. That is fine. Knowing what the number means helps you understand why a pair feels fast or grippy, and it helps you decide later what to buy if you take up skating regularly.

## A short checklist before you buy

If you are shopping for your own skates, these questions will help.

Where will you skate most? If the answer is mostly a smooth indoor floor, look at the harder indoor range. If it is mostly outdoors, look at softer wheels.

How new are you? If you are just starting, a softer wheel can give you more grip while you learn.

Do you want to skate in both places? Then ask the seller about wheels made for more than one surface, and ask which one the skates ship with. A shop can tell you the hardness of the wheels on any pair you are looking at.

Do you know what you have now? If you already own skates, find the hardness rating for your wheels and compare it with the ranges above.

## The short version

Durometer is the hardness rating on a skate wheel, written on the A scale. Lower is softer and grips more. Higher is harder and rolls faster. Outdoor wheels tend to be softer, and indoor wheels tend to be harder. Pick the wheel for the floor you will skate on, and start soft if you are new.
